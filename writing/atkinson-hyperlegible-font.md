Earlier in the month I set out to find a main font for this site. I knew I wanted something easy on
the eyes, and easier on the brain. I found my answer in
[Atkinson Hyperlegible fonts](https://www.brailleinstitute.org/freefont/). The font "focuses on
letterform distinction" and is designed to help low-vision readers better decode text. Techniques
that enhance letterform distinction include:

<!-- prettier-ignore-start -->
- Unambiguous Letterforms (think "8" vs "B" or "O" vs "0")
- Spacious counters (the enclosed areas inside letters)
- Angled spurs (small projections from the main strokes) and longer tails
{.bulleted-list}

<!-- prettier-ignore-end -->

An
[article in Dezeen Magazine](https://www.dezeen.com/2020/09/11/atkinson-hyperlegible-typeface-applied-design-works/)
calls the letterforms "distinct and exaggerated". To the typographer this may be so, but I find them
beautifully generic on the surface and well suited for ordinary copy.

The first Atkinson Hyperlegible font was designed in 2019 by
[Applied Design Works](https://helloapplied.com/) and the
[Braille Institute](https://www.brailleinstitute.org/). The name "Atkinson" pays homage to
[Robert Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Robert_Atkinson), the founder of the Braille
Institute. As of today, two siblings of the original Atkinson Hyperlegible are available: "Next" and
"Mono". The first boasts more weight options than the original, and the second is monospaced.

It is surprising to me that the concept of "hyperlegibility" should be so new. You would think
something so sensible as a font optimized for universal readability would have been designed
generations ago. I applaud Applied Design Works and the Braille Institute, and am happy their
hyperlegible font has been added to the Cooper Hewitt, Smithsonian Design Museum's permanent
collection.
