# What is Universal Design?

<blockquote>
  <p>The design of products and environments to be usable by all people, to the greatest extent
possible, without the need for specialized design.</p>
  <footer>-- <cite>The Center for Universal Design</cite></footer>
</blockquote>

The concept of Universal Design as we know it today has its roots at North Carolina State
University.\* In 1989, Ronald L. Mace, an accessibility activist, architect, and alumnus of the
university, founded the Center for Accessible Housing. It eventually became the
<a href="https://design.ncsu.edu/research/center-for-universal-design/">Center for Universal
Design</a>. Here, the seven Principles of Universal Design were created. Version 2, published in
April of 1997, remains the current version.

The seven principles help designers and the public at large better understand what makes things more
usable. The core principles are:

<ol class="principles-list">
  <li>Equitable Use</li>
  <li>Flexibility in Use</li>
  <li>Simple and Intuitive Use</li>
  <li>Perceptible Information</li>
  <li>Tolerance for Error</li>
  <li>Low Physical Effort</li>
  <li>Size and Space for Approach and Use</li>
</ol>

These principles have remained unchanged for nearly thirty years. Their resilience is a testament to
their fundamental and comprehensive nature. Also impressive is their transferability, as they were
conceived in the realm of architectural design and now are referenced from other realms, such as
digital accessibility. As an illustration, consider how the seven principles might map to the four
WCAG 2.0 "POUR" principles (2008):

Every Universal Design principle may map to a POUR principle, with the exception of "size and space
for approach and use", which is more exclusive to architecture. Likewise, every POUR principle may
map to a Universal Design principle, with the exception of "robust", which is more exclusive to
digital accessibility (in the table, the mappings for "equitable use" are parenthetical because
rather than neatly mapping to one or more POUR principles, "equitable use" is integral to the POUR
principles' general purpose).

So, "Universal Design" is a term of sound definition, born from the world of architectural design,
that has been adopted by various disciplines to describe design that maximizes accessibility. The
term has by now been written into international law, including the UN's Convention on the Rights of
Persons with Disabilities (CRPD). Thus, it is a widely-accepted and active term which will likely
see continued and growing use.

_\* Note that the concept of Universal Design is not without preceding or parallel terms. Examples
include "Barrier-free Design", "Design for All", "Inclusive Design", and "Human-centered Design"._
